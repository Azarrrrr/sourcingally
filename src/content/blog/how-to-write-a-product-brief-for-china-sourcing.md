A product brief is a short written description of what you need a supplier to understand. It is not a legal manufacturing contract, and it does not need to answer every question perfectly. Its job is simpler: give suppliers, sourcing support, sample reviewers, and quality teams the same starting point.

Without a brief, each person fills the gaps differently. One supplier may quote a heavier material, another may assume a smaller size, and a third may exclude packaging. The answers can look comparable when they are not.

## The six parts of a useful brief

### 1. Product purpose

Begin with what the product is for and who will use it. This helps a supplier understand the main functional question instead of seeing only a photograph.

For example: “A reusable storage container for household kitchen use” gives a different context from “a container for food-service use” or “a promotional gift set.” If the product must hold weight, resist water, fold, stack, connect to another product, or be used by children, say so.

### 2. Reference material

Include links, photos, drawings, sketches, or an existing sample where possible. Label what each reference is meant to show. A photograph may show the general shape but not the material. A drawing may show dimensions but not color. A product link may show inspiration, not a product you want copied exactly.

If you are using a reference product, explain what should change. The more specific you are, the less likely the conversation turns into guesswork.

### 3. Product requirements

Write the requirements that matter most to the end user. Depending on the product, this could include material, dimensions, color, weight, thickness, component details, function, finish, texture, assembly, power source, or compatibility.

Do not claim requirements you have not checked. If a material is a preference rather than a confirmed requirement, say that. If you need supplier advice on a material choice, make that a question instead of presenting it as a fixed answer.

### 4. Quantity and commercial context

Give a target quantity or range. Suppliers may quote differently at 100, 500, 1,000, or 10,000 units. Mention whether this is an early sample request, a test order, or a repeat purchase plan. Do not ask for an exact final price while leaving the quantity, packaging, or customization unclear.

You can also state your target market, timing, and whether you are planning a branded, private-label, or standard product. These details can change the supplier’s response and the questions they need to ask.

### 5. Packaging and brand treatment

Packaging is often treated as an afterthought. It should be part of the brief if you need a custom box, insert card, barcode, label, logo, instruction sheet, or specific packing method. State whether artwork exists, who will provide it, and whether you need help understanding the supplier’s file requirements.

A good question is: “What packaging information do you need from us before you can quote or sample this product?” That invites a clear response rather than an assumption.

### 6. Open questions

The strongest briefs do not pretend the buyer knows everything. End with the questions you need the supplier or sourcing partner to answer. For example:

- Can you make this product using the material and dimensions described?
- What quantity assumptions are you using for the quotation?
- What would you need to prepare a sample?
- Which parts of the brief are unclear or not feasible?
- What packaging options are available for this product?

This turns the brief into a conversation tool, not just a document.

## A short template

Use this structure when you make your first version:

**Product:** What is it, who is it for, and how will it be used?

**Reference material:** Links, images, drawings, or an existing sample, plus a note about what each one shows.

**Key requirements:** Material, dimensions, colors, function, finish, components, or other requirements.

**Quantity:** Target quantity or a range, plus whether this is a sample, test order, or planned production order.

**Destination and timing:** Where will the product be sold or delivered, and what timing are you considering?

**Packaging / branding:** Box, logo, labels, barcode, insert card, artwork, or special packaging request.

**Questions:** The exact decision you need help making.

## Make the brief easy to compare

When you send the brief to multiple suppliers, do not rewrite it for each person. Keep a core version so the responses can be compared fairly. If a supplier asks a good question and you clarify the requirement, update the brief and send the same clarification to the other suppliers you are considering.

This is especially useful when you are comparing MOQ, samples, materials, production timing, or packaging. A consistent brief lets you see whether the difference is a real supplier difference or simply a different interpretation of the request.

## What a brief cannot do

A brief does not verify a supplier. It does not prove compliance, confirm a final cost, or guarantee that a production batch will match an early sample. It simply reduces confusion and creates a record of what was discussed.

For higher-risk products, customized products, or destination-market requirements, you may need current input from a qualified testing, legal, customs, engineering, logistics, or other professional adviser. The written brief makes those conversations easier too.
