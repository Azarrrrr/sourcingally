A sourcing process is not a straight line from product idea to payment. It is a chain of decisions. The aim is not to eliminate every risk; it is to make the important questions visible early enough to act on them.

## Stage 1: Define the requirement

Before you search for suppliers, write what you want to make or buy. Include the product purpose, user, quantity range, materials or features that matter, destination, timing, references, packaging needs, and unknowns. A short brief can be enough to begin. A vague brief produces vague quotes.

The key question at this stage is: **what must be true for this product to be acceptable?**

## Stage 2: Research and shortlist suppliers

Supplier research should be structured around the product brief. You may use online platforms, trade shows, referrals, domestic Chinese channels with local help, or direct research. The source is less important than the questions you ask.

Compare suppliers against the same request. Record the version quoted, MOQ, price assumptions, sample approach, communication quality, production questions, packaging questions, and next action. Do not assume that a company profile or product listing proves it can make your exact product.

## Stage 3: Request and review a sample

A sample helps answer whether the supplier understands the brief and whether the product performs as intended. Inspect the aspects that matter to the customer: dimensions, materials, finish, function, assembly, color, packaging, and weak points. Record the version and all feedback.

If you change material, dimensions, packaging, or branding after the sample, make a new written record. A later production problem is often connected to an earlier change that was never captured clearly.

## Stage 4: Confirm the production version

Before production begins, keep the current product requirements visible. This may include the approved sample, a specification sheet, artwork, packaging file, quantity, quality checklist, label questions, timing, and the responsible contact for each part.

This is the stage where a project moves from an idea to a controlled instruction. The more customized the product, the more important this record becomes.

## Stage 5: Follow production and check evidence

The right level of production follow-up depends on the product and risk. A simple order may only need clear communication and a final check. A customized, higher-value, or higher-risk order may need more frequent confirmation. The purpose is not to create constant messages; it is to verify the facts that matter.

A factory or supplier check can help answer local questions about a visible operation, a sample, a product status, or a specific concern. A quality inspection focuses on finished goods or an agreed production stage. Each activity should have a written scope.

## Stage 6: Inspect finished goods and packaging

A final check should connect to the actual product brief. Determine what will be checked: quantity, appearance, function, dimensions, assembly, packaging, labels, or other agreed points. Define important tolerances and defects before the check takes place.

An inspection can identify a problem. It does not automatically tell you the correct commercial or legal response. Keep the evidence, compare it to the agreed requirement, and decide the next action with the right parties.

## Stage 7: Prepare shipment and handover

Before goods move, confirm the product, quantity, packaging, relevant documents, transport responsibility, handover timing, and destination-market questions. Shipping, customs, testing, and product compliance requirements are specific to the actual product and route. Obtain current advice for the real shipment.

Do not wait until the final day to ask who is responsible for freight, documents, labels, or the importer role. Those questions can affect earlier packaging and product decisions.

## Stage 8: Keep the record for the next order

After the first order, keep the approved specification, sample history, supplier messages, quality notes, packaging files, and shipment documents organized. A repeat order is much easier when the current product version is clear.

The practical test is simple: could a new person joining the project understand what was approved, what changed, and what needs to happen next? If not, the project record needs more structure.
