## Why multiple quotes rarely mean the same thing

If you’ve requested quotes from several Chinese suppliers and the numbers are all over the place, you are not alone. A quote is a response to a set of assumptions—both yours and the supplier’s. Unless you defined every commercial and technical parameter in writing and verified each supplier quoted the same basis, you are not looking at directly comparable offers.

Even when your RFQ looks clear, suppliers fill gaps differently: choosing a resin grade they know, rounding a tolerance they typically use, quoting ex-works when you expected delivered, or pricing for a smaller master carton. None of this is bad faith. It is how quoting works when requirements are partially implied.

The fix is practical: document a comparison sheet that turns assumptions into line items. Then normalize every quote to the same written requirement before you compare price.

## Build a side-by-side comparison sheet

Create a single-page sheet for each part or SKU and use it to record what each supplier actually priced. You can maintain this in a spreadsheet. The aim is not to catch anyone out—it’s to eliminate hidden differences.

Include at least these fields:

- Product version and drawing/date
  - The exact revision, file name, and date. If you sent a 3D file and a PDF, note both.
- Material and grade
  - Resin/alloy/cloth type; specific grade number; shore hardness; finish; coating; adhesive system. Note any allowed equivalents.
- Dimensions and tolerances
  - Critical-to-quality dimensions, GD&T, flatness/straightness, weight range. Capture whether the supplier quoted nominal only or included tolerances.
- Unit basis for price
  - Per piece, per set, per meter/kg, per assembled kit? Minimum priceable increment? Include price break tiers and whether the quote assumes a single mixed-color run or one color per run.
- Minimum order quantity (MOQ)
  - By color/size/version; per order or per shipment; whether MOQ ties to price tier.
- Tooling, fixtures, or setup
  - Mold count, cavity, tool steel, ownership, storage, maintenance, and lead time. Note whether setup/changeover fees apply per run.
- Sample/prototype cost and terms
  - Cost, number of iterations included, lead time, refundable or not, and whether samples are from production tooling.
- Packaging
  - Inner pack, master carton, protective inserts, bag type and thickness, desiccants/rust inhibitors, palletization, carton test standard if any.
- Labeling and documentation
  - Barcodes, carton marks, country-of-origin label, instruction manuals, language requirements, any provided artwork formats.
- Testing and compliance responsibilities
  - Who will arrange and pay for product tests, inspections, and any conformity assessments. Specify scope if known.
- Quality checkpoints
  - First-article approval, in-process checks, pre-shipment inspection criteria, acceptable quality limits (AQLs) if used.
- Lead time definition and number
  - Start point (deposit, drawing sign-off, tool approval), and end point (ex-works ready date or delivered to named place). Separate new-tooling lead time from repeat orders.
- Payment milestones
  - Deposit percentage, balance timing, and any progress payments (e.g., upon tool T1, T2). Specify currency.
- Delivery term (Incoterms 2020)
  - Exact Incoterms 2020 rule (e.g., FCA Shenzhen, FOB Yantian, DAP Chicago) with the named place. Incoterms clarify allocation of costs, risk, and obligations between buyer and seller; there are 11 rules in Incoterms 2020. They do not by themselves settle price, payment timing, ownership transfer, product conformity, liability for delay, or how disputes are handled. - Quote validity
  - Valid-through date and conditions that can trigger re-quote (e.g., material index movement).
- Exclusions and assumptions
  - Anything the supplier says is not included: jigs, special packaging, brand licensing, rush fees, customs clearance, duties, or domestic delivery.
- Open questions
  - Ambiguities you need to resolve before you compare or commit.

When you receive a quote, populate every field you can from the supplier’s documents and your email trail. Where a field is blank, mark it clearly as “Not stated” and ask for detail.

## Compare by written requirement, not by price column

A clean “apples-to-apples” comparison only becomes possible after you lock a written baseline:

- Compile a concise product requirement sheet
  - One page per SKU with the exact spec: drawing revision, material grade, finish, packaging bill of materials, labeling, testing scope, quality checkpoints, and delivery term.
- Circulate the same requirement to all shortlisted suppliers
  - Ask each to confirm any deviations in writing—preferably by marking up your sheet (redlines).
- Resolve conflicts once, in writing
  - If Supplier A can hold ±0.1 mm but Supplier B proposes ±0.2 mm, decide which spec you will buy. Update the sheet and resend for confirmation.
- Normalize the quotes
  - Where suppliers quoted different terms (e.g., one included inner trays, others didn’t), add or subtract comparable costs or ask for requotes to the same baseline.

Your goal is not to push the lowest number possible but to knowingly choose the combination of price, capability, lead time, and risk that fits your plan.

## How to spot an incomplete quote (without assuming bad intent)

Most “too good to be true” quotes are simply under-scoped. Here are common flags:

- Unnamed materials or grades
  - “PP” or “stainless steel” without a grade number leaves plenty of room for substitution.
- Vague packaging
  - “Standard export carton” can vary from single-wall to heavy double-wall with inserts. If you care about drop or compression resistance, specify.
- Missing tooling details
  - A line that reads “Tooling included” without cavity count, steel type, ownership, and maintenance terms is incomplete.
- Undefined compliance/testing
  - If your product will require testing or certification, a quote that is silent on who does what is not complete.
- Delivery term without a named place
  - “FOB China” is not an Incoterms 2020 location; “FOB Yantian” is. Ambiguity here often masks uncounted local charges.
- Lead time without start/end definition
  - “25 days” can mean after deposit, after sample approval, or after material receipt. Pin it down.
- Non-standard MOQs for complex builds
  - Very low MOQs on multi-step assemblies often imply parts of the scope (like custom colorants or specific packing) were not included.

When you see one or more of these, pause. Ask for specificity, not discounts.

## When to pause and request clarification

Hit pause and send a short, written clarification request when:

- Two quotes differ by more than 20% and you cannot explain the gap by scope.
- Any supplier did not acknowledge the latest drawing or revision.
- The Incoterms 2020 rule or named place is missing, or differs across quotes.
- Tooling terms are materially different (e.g., “rental” vs. “buyer-owned”).
- MOQ, pack-out, or labeling differs from your RFQ.
- Lead times start from different milestones.
- A supplier refuses to specify a material grade or tolerance.

Polite, specific questions such as “Please confirm the exact PP grade (e.g., PP-H YYY) used for this price” are faster to resolve than generic “please confirm” notes.

## Delivery terms matter: use Incoterms 2020 precisely

Incoterms 2020 are internationally recognized rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce to clarify buyer and seller responsibilities and to allocate cost, risk, and obligations through the delivery chain. There are 11 rules in Incoterms 2020, and each one defines where risk transfers and who pays for which parts of the move. According to the U.S. International Trade Administration and the ICC, Incoterms help you and the supplier understand logistics responsibilities, but they do not themselves determine your price, payment timing, transfer of ownership, whether the product conforms to spec, liability for delays, or how disputes are resolved. Those must be addressed elsewhere in your purchase order or contract.

Action points:
- Always specify the Incoterms 2020 rule and the named place (e.g., FCA Shenzhen warehouse; FOB Ningbo; DDP 12345 USA).
- Align your lead time definition with the term (e.g., “25 days to FOB Ningbo” vs. “25 days to DDP Dallas”).
- Ensure your comparison sheet uses the same delivery term across suppliers or you adjust for the difference.

## What not to conclude from a quote

A quote is not a contract and not proof of capability. Avoid these common leaps:

- “The lowest quote means the same spec at a better margin.” It may reflect a lighter pack, wider tolerance, or different grade.
- “The supplier can meet our tolerance because they priced it.” Capability requires evidence (sample results, process notes), not just a number.
- “FOB means they will deliver to my warehouse.” It does not; in Incoterms 2020, FOB risk transfers at loading on the vessel at the named port.
- “A short lead time means they have capacity reserved.” It usually assumes fast approvals and material availability; confirm both.
- “The quote includes all compliance.” Unless the quote lists specific tests and responsibilities, assume nothing.

## Turn due diligence into a decision record

Beyond comparing numbers, keep a written log of what you checked, what you decided, and why. As the U.S. International Trade Administration notes, performing due diligence helps protect your company from problems, loss, and liability, and it should continue as your business expands. Treat your decision record as part of that ongoing diligence.

What to capture:
- Supplier identity checks
  - Legal name, registration, site address, ownership, basic financial stability notes.
- Scope confirmations
  - The finalized comparison sheet each supplier accepted, including any deviations.
- Evidence of capability
  - Photos or videos of similar parts, sample measurement reports, process notes, references (when available).
- Risk items and mitigations
  - Tolerance challenges, surface finish risks, color stability, packaging robustness—and how you plan to verify them.
- Sample and test outcomes
  - First-article approvals, dimensional reports, packaging drop/compression results if conducted.
- Commercial terms
  - Final Incoterms 2020 rule and named place, payment milestones, quote validity, and any agreed change-management process.
- Communication history
  - Key clarifications and commitments made in writing.

This record will help you explain choices to colleagues, onboard backups if needed, and revisit assumptions after first delivery. It does not guarantee outcomes, but it raises your odds of managing them.

## A practical workflow to compare quotes

Use this as your baseline process from RFQ to selection:

1) Freeze the baseline
- Publish a one-page requirement sheet per SKU.
- State the required Incoterms 2020 rule and named place.
- Define how lead time will be measured.

2) Collect and log quotes
- Paste each quote’s data into your comparison sheet fields.
- Flag every blank or ambiguous field as “Not stated.”

3) Normalize scope
- Ask each supplier to confirm or redline your requirement sheet.
- Request requotes where scope changed.

4) Validate capability on the key risks
- Ask for process notes on critical tolerances or finishes.
- If feasible, order one pre-production sample or a material coupon for testing.

5) Compare total landed basis (if applicable)
- If delivery terms differ, adjust for logistics so you compare like-for-like. Or ask for all quotes on the same term.

6) Review commercial fit
- Assess MOQs versus your forecast, payment milestones against cash flow, and quote validity versus decision timing.

7) Decide and document
- Select primary and, if prudent, a backup supplier.
- Store the final comparison, clarifications, and commitments in your decision record.

## An actionable checklist before you choose

- Confirm the exact product version and drawing revision on every quote.
- Write the material grade, finish, and any allowable substitutes—do not accept generic labels.
- Ensure all critical tolerances are stated, not implied.
- Align the unit basis (per piece/set/meter/kg) and pack-out across quotes.
- Verify MOQ by color/size/version; check if it drives the unit price.
- Lock the tooling scope, ownership, maintenance, and lead time in writing.
- Specify sample cost, iteration count, and whether samples come from production tooling.
- Define packaging in detail, including protective materials and carton specs.
- Assign testing/compliance responsibilities; list exact tests if known.
- Align lead time start and end points; separate NPI from repeat order timing.
- Set payment milestones and currency; avoid open-ended “balance before shipment” without timing.
- State Incoterms 2020 rule with named place; ensure comparability across quotes.
- Record quote validity and triggers for re-quote (e.g., material index changes).
- List exclusions/assumptions; convert critical ones into inclusions or decisions.
- Write down open questions; resolve them before selection.

## How to communicate clarifications efficiently

- Be specific and bounded
  - “Please confirm 5052-H32 alloy for the quoted price” beats “Please confirm alloy.”
- Use numbered questions
  - Makes answers easier to track and reduces partial replies.
- Reference your requirement sheet
  - “See Section 3: Packaging — confirm double-wall master carton, 5-ply.”
- Set a response window
  - “Kindly reply within two business days so we can compare fairly.”
- Close the loop
  - When you receive answers, update the comparison sheet and your requirement baseline.

## Manage changes before they manage you

Prices and lead times move with materials and capacity. Reduce surprise by:

- Capturing quote validity and any indexation
  - If a supplier references a resin index, note how and when it applies.
- Defining how engineering changes are handled
  - Who quotes, who approves, how tooling or process changes are validated.
- Planning for verification
  - Schedule first-article checks, packaging tests, or small pilot runs before full release.

## When to ask for local support

If you are short on time, dealing with complex assemblies, or struggling to reconcile conflicting quotes, a local sourcing partner can help you tighten the requirement sheet, translate technical and commercial nuances, coordinate comparable quotes on the same Incoterms 2020 basis, organize sample builds, and keep a clean decision record. They can also perform on-site observations, consolidate clarifications, and follow up with factories during early production. This kind of support does not guarantee outcomes, but it can reduce avoidable misalignment and help you act on clearer information.
