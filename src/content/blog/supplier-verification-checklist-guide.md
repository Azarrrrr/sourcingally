Supplier verification is often described as a yes-or-no test: verified or not verified. In real sourcing work, it is more useful to think in layers. Each check can answer a different question. A company registration detail may help identify the entity you are speaking with. A sample may show whether the supplier understands the product. A factory visit may show visible operations. None of these alone proves every future outcome.

## Layer 1: Basic identity and contact information

Start by recording the supplier’s company name, address, contact person, website or marketplace listing, product category, and the source where you found them. Ask whether the quoted product is made by the company itself, sourced through another company, or handled through a trading arrangement.

The purpose is not to make a legal conclusion from a website. It is to make sure you can identify who is answering your request and what role they say they play.

## Layer 2: Product-specific capability

A company may make products in a broad category without being suited to your exact requirement. Ask whether it can make the specific material, dimensions, function, packaging, quantity, or customization you need. Ask what information it needs to confirm feasibility.

This layer is especially important for customized products. A supplier may be able to produce a similar item but not your desired variation without different tooling, materials, MOQ, or process changes.

## Layer 3: Communication and quotation quality

Communication is evidence, although it is not proof of manufacturing quality. Does the supplier respond to the actual brief? Does it identify missing information? Does it state quantity, material, packaging, and timing assumptions? Can you tell which product version is being discussed?

A clear response makes later verification easier. A vague response is a signal that you may need more questions before moving on.

## Layer 4: Samples

A sample can show whether the supplier has understood the product request and can produce a version that you can evaluate. Test the sample for relevant dimensions, materials, function, finish, customer use, and packaging. Record the version and the feedback.

A sample does not guarantee that all mass-produced goods will match it. It is one stage in a process that should later include a clear production record and, where appropriate, quality checks.

## Layer 5: Factory or supplier visit

A local visit can help confirm visible facts such as the location, visible production environment, equipment, staff activity, product process, or the status of a specific sample or order. The visit should have a written purpose. For example: “confirm whether this supplier has visible capability to perform the product process described in the brief” is more useful than “check if the factory is good.”

A visit cannot guarantee future production quality, legal compliance, financial condition, supply continuity, or every subcontracting arrangement. The report should make clear what was observed, what was not observed, and what remains unknown.

## Layer 6: Production and quality evidence

Before shipment, a product-specific check may focus on quantity, appearance, function, measurements, packaging, labels, or agreed tolerances. This evidence is different from a factory-identity check. Keep the scope tied to the approved brief.

## A working checklist

For each potential supplier, write down:

1. Identity and contact information.
2. Exact product version discussed.
3. Product-specific capability questions and answers.
4. Quote assumptions: quantity, material, packaging, timing, and customization.
5. Sample status and feedback.
6. Factory-visit question, if one is needed.
7. Quality-control requirements before shipment.
8. Open questions and the next owner.

The value of this checklist is not in having more columns. It is in preventing assumptions from becoming decisions.
