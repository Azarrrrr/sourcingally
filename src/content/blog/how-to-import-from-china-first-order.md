Importing from China is often described as a search problem: find a product, find a supplier, ask for a price. That is only the first layer. A better first-order process is a decision system. You need to know what you are buying, what is still unknown, what evidence you need before paying, and who is responsible for each step.

This guide is for a buyer preparing a first product order. It is not legal, customs, tax, testing, safety, or freight advice for a particular shipment. Requirements can change with the product, destination, importer, transport route, and commercial terms. Use the framework to prepare better questions, then get current professional advice for the actual order.

## Start with a product brief, not a supplier search

A supplier cannot quote or sample a product reliably if the request is only “I want something like this.” You do not need a perfect technical specification on day one, but you should write down the facts you already know.

A useful first brief includes the product’s intended customer and use; reference images or links; the quantity you are considering; material, size, color, or functional requirements; packaging and branding needs; the destination market; the expected timing; and the decision you need help making. If you do not know one answer, write that down too. Unknown is better than assumed.

For example, a buyer asking for “a silicone kitchen product” may receive quotations for very different materials, thicknesses, packaging assumptions, and order quantities. A short written brief gives each supplier the same starting point. That makes later comparison much more meaningful.

## Choose the question you need a supplier to answer

The first supplier conversation should not try to solve everything at once. Decide what you are trying to learn. Is it whether the supplier can make the exact product? Whether the requested material is available? Whether a logo changes the minimum order quantity? Whether a sample can be prepared? Or whether a visit is needed to clarify production capability?

This matters because different questions require different evidence. A product listing, chat response, sample, factory visit, packaging proof, and final inspection all answer different questions. Do not treat one as a replacement for the others.

## Compare consistent information

If you contact several suppliers, send the same written brief and ask the same core questions. Ask about the product version quoted, MOQ, unit price at relevant quantities, sample approach, tooling or customization assumptions, production lead time, packaging, and the exact information the supplier still needs.

A lower quotation is not automatically a better quotation. It can reflect a different material, a different quantity, less packaging, an incomplete scope, or an assumption you did not notice. The comparison should show what is included and what remains uncertain.

A simple supplier comparison sheet is often more useful than dozens of messages. Use one row per supplier and include columns for product fit, response quality, sample plan, price assumptions, MOQ, lead time, packaging, factory verification question, and next action.

## Treat the sample as a decision point

A sample is not just a photo opportunity. It is a chance to test whether the product matches the written brief and whether you and the supplier understand the same thing.

Check the sample in the way a customer would use it. Compare dimensions, material, color, function, assembly, finish, smell where relevant, packaging, and the parts most likely to fail or create returns. Record the sample version. Write feedback in a way that can be checked: “move the logo 5 mm lower” is more useful than “the logo looks wrong.”

If the product will have customized packaging, treat packaging as part of the sample process. A correct product in the wrong package is still a problem.

## Plan quality before production is complete

Quality control works best when you define what quality means for the actual product. That may include appearance, quantity, dimensions, color, function, assembly, packaging, labels, and acceptable defect criteria. The list does not need to be complicated. It does need to be written before a final check happens.

Depending on the product and project, useful checkpoints may include a sample review, a production-stage check, and a final-product inspection. Each stage has a different purpose. A factory or supplier check can help you understand the visible operation and answer specific local questions. A final inspection focuses on finished goods against the agreed requirements. Neither step guarantees every future outcome.

## Prepare shipping questions early

Shipping should not be the first thing you decide, but it should not be the last either. Before goods are ready, confirm who is responsible for transport, what commercial terms apply, which documents are needed, how packaging will protect the goods, and what destination-market questions require current professional input.

The product, packaging, quantity, value, route, importer role, and destination all affect shipment planning. Do not rely on a generic online answer for compliance or customs treatment. Build a list of the questions your freight provider, customs adviser, tester, or other qualified professional needs to answer for your real shipment.

## Keep a written decision record

A sourcing project becomes difficult when the current version of the product is unclear. Keep a simple record of the approved sample, product changes, packaging file, quotation version, open questions, owner, and next date. This does not need special software. A well-organized folder and a clear spreadsheet can be enough for a first order.

The important part is that everyone can identify the same version of the product and the same next action.

## A short first-order checklist

Before you move from research to an order, make sure you can answer these questions:

1. What exactly is the product, and what does the customer need it to do?
2. Which parts of the product brief are confirmed, and which are still unknown?
3. Are you comparing suppliers against the same request?
4. What will the sample need to prove?
5. What will be checked before shipment?
6. What packaging, labeling, or document questions are open?
7. Who is responsible for each next action?

## The practical takeaway

A first China order does not need a perfect plan. It needs a clearer sequence. Define the product, compare consistent information, use samples as evidence, set the quality questions early, prepare shipment questions before handover, and keep the current decision visible. That approach gives you a much stronger starting point than chasing the fastest quote.
