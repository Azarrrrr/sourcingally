## A public holiday is not the same as your supplier’s production schedule

Buyers often ask whether a Chinese holiday will “delay the order.” The more useful question is: **which part of this project will be affected, at this supplier, on this route, at this point in the schedule?**

A public holiday can affect supplier communication, factory staffing, sample approvals, production handovers, trucking, warehouse receiving, export documents, freight capacity, and the time needed to clear a backlog after work resumes. The effect is not identical for every company, product, port, or shipment.

That is why a calendar date alone is not a reliable sourcing plan. Use the official holiday calendar as a planning signal, then confirm the actual closure and restart plan with each supplier and logistics provider involved in the order.

China publishes its annual public-holiday arrangements through official channels.[1] Freight providers often issue separate operational guidance because a holiday period can create pressure before the break and recovery delays after it, even when a port or airport remains open.[2]

## The three dates that matter more than the holiday date

For every meaningful holiday period, record three supplier-specific dates:

1. **Last normal working date.** The last day on which the supplier can reliably confirm changes, run normal production, arrange a sample, or prepare cargo.
2. **Actual closure and staffing period.** A factory may stop production before the statutory break, operate with reduced staffing, or restart in stages.
3. **Stable restart date.** The date when the relevant team, materials, production line, warehouse, and logistics contacts are genuinely back to normal capacity.

These dates may differ across the factory, a packaging supplier, a testing provider, a forwarder, and a destination warehouse. The project schedule should reflect the slowest critical step, not the most optimistic answer.

## Where holiday pressure enters a sourcing project

A holiday does not only affect final shipment. It can interrupt the project earlier than expected.

| Project stage | Holiday-related question to confirm |
|---|---|
| Supplier research | Will the responsible commercial and technical contacts be available to answer the brief? |
| Sampling | Can the supplier make, revise, ship, and discuss the sample before the closure? |
| Tooling or development | Which milestone must be approved before work pauses, and what happens if feedback arrives late? |
| Mass production | When is the last material release, production date, packing date, and finished-goods date before the break? |
| Inspection | Will the goods be complete and accessible when the inspection is planned? |
| Trucking and warehouse handover | Are pickup appointments, warehouse receiving times, and drivers confirmed before the last working window? |
| Freight | Is space actually confirmed, and what are the booking and cargo cutoffs? |
| Documents and payment | Are the invoice, packing list, certificates, approvals, and payment steps complete before the relevant offices reduce activity? |

The key is to make each dependency visible. A factory can finish an order, for example, while the shipment still waits because packaging is incomplete, cargo cannot be picked up, or documents are not ready.

## Why pre-holiday periods can be harder than the holiday itself

The final days before a major break can be the least forgiving part of the schedule. Suppliers may be trying to complete several orders. Trucking and warehouse appointments can tighten. Export teams may have more document requests. Buyers may push for last-minute changes to get cargo out before the break.

The result is a common mistake: treating a stated cargo-ready date as if it were a confirmed departure date.

Separate these milestones in your records:

- Production complete
- Quality check complete
- Packaging complete
- Goods ready for pickup
- Pickup completed
- Cargo delivered to the agreed handover point
- Booking confirmed
- Cutoff met
- Departure confirmed

A shipment that misses any one of these may move after the holiday instead. That is not necessarily a failure by any one party; it is a reason to plan the handover as carefully as the production schedule.

## Plan backwards from the decision that matters

Do not begin with a generic buffer such as “add two weeks.” The amount of time needed depends on the product, supplier, order size, production stage, delivery term, transport mode, destination requirements, and how many open decisions remain.

Instead, start with the delivery or inventory date that matters to your business. Then work backwards through the actual decision chain:

1. When must the goods arrive or be available for sale?
2. What transport time and destination handling time should be planned for the chosen route?
3. What is the last reliable handover date before the holiday pressure period?
4. When must inspection, packaging, labels, and documents be completed?
5. When must production be complete?
6. When must the sample, material, artwork, purchase order, or deposit be approved for production to begin?

This method does not remove uncertainty. It makes the uncertainty explicit, so you can decide whether to move an order earlier, split a shipment, simplify a change, or accept a later delivery date.

## Confirm supplier schedules in writing

Send a short, specific planning message rather than asking only, “Will you close for the holiday?”

> Please confirm your planned closure dates, last normal production date, final date for sample or artwork approval, finished-goods date for this order, final pickup date, and expected stable restart date. Please also identify any material, packaging, staffing, or logistics risks that could affect the quoted schedule.

Ask the same question of each supplier whose output is needed for the finished order. If your product uses custom packaging, labels, inserts, or multiple components, one supplier’s closure can hold the entire shipment.

Record the answers in the same project sheet as the product brief, quote assumptions, sample status, and shipment plan. The [supplier communication guide](/en/blog/evaluate-supplier-communication-before-order/) explains why a written, version-controlled record matters before and during an order.

## Keep changes away from the final working window

A late change can be manageable in ordinary conditions and expensive before a holiday. A new logo placement, revised carton artwork, material substitution, or additional test request can affect production, packing, documentation, and pickup at the same time.

Before a major break, distinguish between:

- **Critical changes** that are necessary for product safety, legal compliance, function, or contractual accuracy.
- **Important but deferrable changes** that can wait for the next production run or a later shipment.
- **Cosmetic or optional changes** that should not disrupt an otherwise ready order.

There is no universal rule for which changes are acceptable. The point is to understand the consequences in writing before requesting them. Ask the supplier what the change affects: cost, MOQ, tooling, sample approval, lead time, packaging, quality checks, or shipping date.

## Use a defined inspection and document checkpoint

If you plan a final inspection, do not schedule it based only on the supplier’s estimated completion date. Confirm that enough goods will be complete, packed, and available for the agreed scope of inspection. Clarify who will receive the report, who can approve corrective action, and what happens if a problem is found close to a logistics cutoff.

The same discipline applies to documents. Before the final working window, confirm the party responsible for:

- Commercial invoice and packing list
- Carton marks and labels
- Product or origin documents, if applicable
- Booking instructions and delivery order
- Payment milestones
- Destination-market information that must be available before shipment

The [shipping planning guide](/en/blog/how-to-plan-shipping-from-china/) is a useful starting point, but each shipment still needs current guidance from the parties handling the route and import requirements.

## Plan for recovery, not only closure

The first day after a holiday is not always a normal working day in practice. Teams may be clearing backlogs, workers may return at different times, and logistics providers may be working through accumulated pickup and handover requests.

Your post-holiday plan should include:

- A dated supplier restart confirmation
- Updated production and cargo-ready dates
- Confirmation that materials and packaging are available
- Reconfirmed freight booking or next available option
- A check of any missed cutoffs or rolled cargo
- A revised customer or internal delivery expectation if necessary

Treat the restart as a checkpoint. It is an opportunity to refresh the schedule with current information instead of relying on the forecast made before the break.

## A practical holiday-planning checklist

Before any significant Chinese public holiday period, make sure you can answer these questions:

- What are the official holiday dates for the relevant year?
- What are each supplier’s actual last working date, closure period, and stable restart date?
- Which sample, tooling, material, packaging, or approval decision must happen before the break?
- Is the product complete, checked, packed, and ready before the relevant pickup cutoff?
- Are freight space and handover capacity confirmed, not merely requested?
- Are documents and payment steps complete before reduced staffing affects them?
- What is the backup plan if cargo misses the pre-holiday window?
- Who will update the schedule after operations resume?

A holiday calendar should not surprise a well-documented project. It should prompt a better sequence of questions.
