A sample is a decision tool. It gives you something real to compare with the product brief before a full production batch is complete. That makes it one of the most useful parts of a sourcing project—if you treat it as evidence rather than a quick approval step.

## What a sample can show

A sample can help you assess whether the supplier understood the intended product. It can show the general form, materials, dimensions, color, finish, function, assembly, components, packaging, or other product-specific details. It can also reveal the questions that were missing from the original brief.

A sample cannot automatically prove that every production unit will match. It does not replace a final quality plan, and it does not answer every destination-market, testing, legal, or customer-use question. Its purpose is to reduce uncertainty before production begins.

## Start with the customer-use question

Before the sample arrives, decide how the end customer will use the product. A kitchen item may need to be checked for fit, function, cleaning, material questions, and packaging. A textile item may need sizing, color, finish, stitching, and label checks. An electronic accessory may need compatibility, connection, power, finish, packaging, and current compliance questions.

The test should match the product. A sample that looks good in photographs may still have a function, fit, material, or packaging problem.

## Compare it with the written brief

Use the same brief you sent to the supplier. Check the relevant points one by one. If dimensions matter, measure them. If color matters, compare it in a suitable environment. If assembly matters, assemble it. If packaging matters, inspect the actual package, not only a message describing it.

Write feedback in a way that a supplier can check. “The product feels weak” is difficult to act on. “The lid opens when the container is lifted by the handle” is clearer. When possible, use photos, measurements, or marked-up references to show the issue.

## Record the sample version

Every sample should have a visible version or date. Keep the current product photos, supplier information, feedback, changes requested, and the response together. If a new sample is made, record what changed and why.

This avoids one of the most common production problems: a buyer believes the supplier is making the latest approved version, while the supplier is working from an earlier file or a different interpretation.

## Know when a second sample is useful

A second sample may be useful when the first sample has a material, function, dimension, packaging, or major appearance issue. It may also be useful when a design change affects the way the product works. The decision should be based on what the first sample still does not prove.

Do not request repeated samples without a clear feedback record. Each cycle should answer a specific question and move the project toward an approved version.

## Bring packaging into the sample process

If the product needs custom packaging, barcode labels, logo placement, inserts, or special protection, plan those elements before the final production stage. Packaging affects product presentation, customer use, freight handling, and sometimes MOQ or timing.

You do not always need a fully finished retail package with the first functional sample. But you should know when packaging decisions need to be made and how they will be checked.

## A practical sample checklist

Before you approve a sample, ask:

1. Does it match the current written product brief?
2. Does it work as the customer will use it?
3. Are material, dimensions, finish, color, and components acceptable?
4. Are packaging and brand details clear enough for the next stage?
5. What exact version is this sample?
6. What must change before production begins?
7. What will be checked again during or after production?
