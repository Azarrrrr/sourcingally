## You do not need a perfect brief. You need an honest starting point.

Many first-time buyers delay contacting suppliers because they believe every technical detail must be finished first. Others send a product photo and ask for “your best price,” then receive vague quotes that cannot be compared.

The better approach sits between those two extremes. Prepare enough information for a supplier to understand the product, the commercial question, and the uncertainties that still need to be solved. A strong first message does not pretend you know everything. It makes it easy for the supplier to identify what is missing.

Before you contact suppliers, aim to answer three questions:

1. **What are you trying to make or buy?**
2. **What decision do you need help with now?**
3. **What facts are confirmed and what remains open?**

That distinction prevents a supplier from treating an early concept as a finished specification—or treating a finished requirement as a casual idea.

## Begin with the product version you mean today

Give the product a working name and version. It can be simple: “Foldable travel bottle, concept V1” or “Black aluminium desk lamp, drawing revision 03.” The point is to create one identifiable reference for the conversation.

Attach what you have:

- Photos, reference links, drawings, sketches, or a physical sample description
- Dimensions that matter to function, packaging, or compatibility
- Known materials, finish, colour, logo, or branding requirements
- The intended use and target user
- Any product feature that must not change
- The destination market, if known

If you are adapting an existing product, say what must be different. For example: a new material, a different power plug, a larger size, custom packaging, a revised colour, or a branded insert card. Suppliers can only quote the version they understand.

## Separate must-haves from preferences

A first brief becomes clearer when you label each requirement by importance.

| Requirement level | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Must-have | The product is not acceptable without it | A stated size range, material restriction, safety feature, or required logo placement |
| Preferred | You want it, but alternatives may be possible | A preferred carton style or a specific finish |
| Open question | You need the supplier’s input before deciding | Whether a material grade, mechanism, or packaging method is practical at your quantity |

This avoids a common problem: a supplier may quietly treat a preference as optional, or treat an early idea as a fixed technical rule. If something is important, state why it is important and ask the supplier to confirm whether it can meet it.

## Provide a realistic quantity range

Quantity affects price, MOQ, materials, packaging, tooling, production planning, and shipping. You do not always need the final number before your first inquiry, but you should provide a realistic range.

For example:

- Trial order: 300–500 units
- First production target: 1,000–2,000 units
- Expected annual requirement: subject to successful launch
- Colour variants: two colours, quantities not yet split

Do not write “small quantity” or “large order” without a number. A supplier may interpret those words very differently from you. Also specify whether your quantity is per SKU, per colour, per size, or total across several variations.

## State the commercial question you actually need answered

The first supplier message should have a purpose. You may be seeking one or more of the following:

- A feasibility view for a custom product
- A quotation for a defined product version
- An MOQ and price-tier discussion
- A sample plan
- A material or process recommendation
- A packaging estimate
- A production and lead-time estimate
- Confirmation of what evidence or documents are available

If you ask for a quote, say what should be included. A number without a product version, material basis, packaging assumption, or delivery term is difficult to use.

A clearer request would be:

> Please quote the attached version V1 for 1,000 and 3,000 units. State the material and finish assumed, MOQ by colour, sample cost and timing, packaging included, tooling or setup cost, production lead time definition, and any points that require clarification before a final quote.

This is not demanding. It gives the supplier a practical format for responding.

## Include packaging and destination early

Packaging is not only a finishing detail. It can change cost, MOQ, carton size, product protection, labelling, freight, and the work needed from more than one supplier.

At minimum, say whether you need:

- Plain export packing or retail packaging
- Custom box, insert, barcode, label, manual, or warning label
- Product protection for fragile, coated, or easily scratched items
- Bundling, kitting, or multiple components in one package
- A destination-market language or packaging requirement

The destination also matters. A supplier may need to know whether the product is intended for the United States, Europe, the United Kingdom, Latin America, the Middle East, or another market before discussing plug types, labels, documentation, testing questions, or shipping expectations.

General sourcing information can help you identify questions. It is not a substitute for current legal, customs, product-safety, testing, or tax advice for a real shipment.

## Decide what you will compare across suppliers

Before you send inquiries, create a small comparison sheet. Otherwise, each reply arrives in a different format and the decision becomes a memory test.

Use consistent fields for:

- Supplier legal and commercial identity
- Product version quoted
- Material and finish
- Quantity and MOQ basis
- Unit price and price tiers
- Sample cost and iterations
- Tooling or setup cost
- Packaging included
- Production lead time and its start point
- Delivery term and named place, if quoted
- Payment assumptions
- Open questions and exclusions

The goal is not to force every supplier into the same answer. It is to see which answers are different and why. Read the [supplier comparison guide](/en/blog/how-to-compare-chinese-suppliers-beyond-lowest-quote/) before treating a low price as a comparable offer.

## Prepare a short question list

Keep your first questions specific enough that they can be answered. For a product inquiry, you might ask:

1. Can you make the attached product version, and what information is missing to confirm feasibility?
2. Which material, finish, and production process are you assuming for the quotation?
3. What is the MOQ by product version, colour, and packaging option?
4. What does the sample process include, and is a revised sample possible?
5. What packaging is included in the quoted price?
6. What is the estimated production lead time, and when does that timing begin?
7. Are tooling, testing, labels, or special packaging excluded from the price?
8. Can you share a clear list of the product points that need confirmation before order?

Do not send a long list only to see whether the supplier can reply quickly. Ask questions that move your product decision forward.

## Keep reference files organized

Name files so that another person can understand which version is current. Avoid sending several attachments called “final.pdf,” “new final.pdf,” and “final final.pdf.”

A simple structure is enough:

- `Product-name_V1_2026-08-14.pdf`
- `Product-name_dimensions_V1.xlsx`
- `Packaging-reference_V1.jpg`
- `Questions-for-supplier_V1.docx`

When you change something, update the version and date. Ask the supplier to confirm which file it used for the quote or sample. This makes it easier to spot a misunderstanding before production begins.

## What you can leave open at the start

Some details cannot be decided until after you speak with suppliers. It is reasonable to leave these open, as long as you label them clearly:

- Exact manufacturing method for a new concept
- Material alternative, if the preferred material is not practical
- Final MOQ after custom colour or packaging requirements are understood
- Tooling approach and ownership terms
- Exact carton configuration
- Production lead time after sample approval
- Whether a second sample or pre-production sample is needed

Do not hide open questions. A good supplier response may help you turn them into decisions. A vague response may show that you need more evidence before proceeding.

## A simple first-message template

Use this as a starting point and adapt it to your product:

> Hello, we are researching suppliers for [product name, version]. Attached are [photos/drawing/specification]. Our target quantity is [range] for [market]. The must-have requirements are [list]. We are still deciding [open questions].
>
> Please confirm whether you can make this version and identify any missing information. For a preliminary quote, please state the material and finish assumed, MOQ, price tiers, sample cost and timing, packaging included, tooling or setup cost, estimated production lead time, and any exclusions. Please also confirm which attachment or version you are using.

It is short enough to use repeatedly and detailed enough to generate a useful response.

## Before you send the first inquiry

Check that you have:

- One identifiable product version
- A reference image, drawing, or description
- A realistic quantity range
- Must-have requirements and known preferences
- Destination-market information, if known
- A packaging starting point
- A short list of questions
- A place to compare supplier replies
- A list of what is still unknown

If you have those basics, you are ready to start a useful conversation. If you do not, build the brief first rather than collecting quotes that cannot be compared.
