A product sample should be reviewed against the same requirements that will matter to the buyer and end customer. The most useful question is not “does this look good?” It is “what does this sample prove, what does it not prove, and what must be changed or confirmed before production?”

## Check the product purpose first

Start by using the product as intended. If it is a storage item, fill, stack, open, and close it. If it is a garment, check sizing, fit, fabric feel, stitching, labels, and care requirements. If it is an electronic accessory, test the functions you expect a customer to use. If it is furniture or a home item, check assembly, stability, surfaces, components, and packing protection.

The exact test depends on the product. A visual review alone is rarely enough.

## Compare dimensions and materials

Use the written brief as the reference. Measure the dimensions that affect use, packaging, or compatibility. Check the material you asked for, where it is visible or can be confirmed. Compare color and finish to your reference. If you cannot verify a material or specification from the sample alone, write that down as an open question rather than assuming it is correct.

A supplier may use a similar-looking material that behaves differently. This is one reason a written material description and sample record are both useful.

## Look at build quality and finish

Inspect seams, edges, joints, fasteners, printing, logos, paint, texture, alignment, moving parts, and surfaces. Look for inconsistent finish, loose parts, visible marks, sharp edges, gaps, stains, or areas that could create a customer-use problem.

Do not turn every minor difference into a rejection. Decide which points are important to the product’s function, brand presentation, or customer expectation. A practical review separates critical issues, improvement requests, and cosmetic preferences.

## Test the packaging experience

If packaging is part of the sample, inspect how the product fits, whether it is protected, whether the print or label is correct, and whether a customer can understand what they receive. Packaging should also be considered for transport: an attractive box that cannot protect the product during shipping may need adjustment.

For private-label products, check logo placement, artwork, barcode space, inserts, labels, and the current file version.

## Create a feedback record

For each issue, record the sample version, a photograph or reference, what you observed, the requested change, and whether the point is critical or optional. Keep the feedback in one document instead of spreading it across many messages.

A clear record may look like this:

| Area | Observation | Requested action | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lid fit | Lid opens when product is lifted | Adjust fit and retest with weight | Critical |
| Logo | Logo sits too close to the edge | Move 5 mm inward | Important |
| Color | Slightly warmer than reference | Send color reference option | Review |

The point is not to create bureaucracy. It is to make sure a supplier can identify the current requirement and that you can tell whether the next sample addressed it.

## Decide whether you are ready to approve

A sample approval should answer more than “I like it.” You should know the exact product version being approved, the points that must stay consistent, the packaging status, the open questions that remain, and the quality checks that will happen later.

If a key issue remains unresolved, ask whether another sample, a written confirmation, a factory discussion, or a different product approach is needed. Approval should be based on the evidence you need for the next decision.
