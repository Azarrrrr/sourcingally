## Start with the right question

Before you pay a supplier deposit, it is sensible to ask a basic question: **which legal entity are you actually dealing with?** That question is narrower than “is this supplier good?” and more useful than a quick website search.

A business-information check can help you match the company named in the quotation, contract, business license, bank instructions, and public registration record. It can reveal a mismatch that needs an explanation before money moves. It cannot, by itself, prove that the company will make your exact product well, deliver on time, remain financially healthy, or meet every future obligation.

Think of this as one layer in a broader supplier-verification process. A company identity check, a product-specific quotation, a sample, a factory visit, and a quality plan answer different questions. Treating any one of them as a complete guarantee creates unnecessary risk.

## Ask for the Chinese legal name, not only an English trading name

The first document to request is a current copy of the supplier’s business license. Chinese company licenses normally contain the entity’s Chinese legal name, Unified Social Credit Code, legal representative, address, business scope, registered capital, date of establishment, and other basic registration information. China Justice Observer explains that companies may use English names commercially, but that the legal Chinese name is the meaningful identity point for the registered entity.[1]

Ask the supplier to provide the following in one written reply:

- Chinese legal company name
- Unified Social Credit Code (USCC)
- Copy of the business license
- Registered address and any actual factory address, if different
- Legal representative’s name
- Business scope shown on the license
- Name of the entity that will issue the invoice and receive payment
- Name and title of the person signing or negotiating for the company

Do not treat an English website name, an Alibaba storefront name, a salesperson’s email signature, or a product catalogue as a legal identity check. They may be perfectly normal commercial materials, but they are not enough to establish whether all documents refer to the same company.

## Match the business license against the public record

China’s National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System is operated by the State Administration for Market Regulation. A practical first step is to compare the information on the supplier’s business license with the record you can find through that system. The public record should match the entity details you received, especially the Chinese company name and Unified Social Credit Code.[1]

Do not stop at a screenshot sent by the supplier. Record what you checked and the date you checked it. A useful comparison sheet has columns for the supplier’s document, the public record, the quotation, the contract, and the payment instructions.

| Item to compare | Why it matters | What to do if it differs |
|---|---|---|
| Chinese legal name | Identifies the contracting entity | Pause and request a written explanation before proceeding. |
| Unified Social Credit Code | Helps distinguish companies with similar names | Recheck every document against the exact code. |
| Registered address | Separates the legal address from any claimed factory location | Ask for the actual operating or factory address and clarify the relationship. |
| Business scope | Gives context about the registered business activities | Ask how the quoted work fits the entity’s stated role. |
| Legal representative | Helps identify the registered authorized representative | Do not assume this person is your everyday contact or contract signatory. |
| Registration status | Helps identify an obvious identity or status issue | Seek current professional advice if the record is unclear or inconsistent. |

This is an identity and consistency exercise, not a legal opinion. If the transaction is substantial, complex, regulated, or difficult to understand, obtain advice from a qualified professional with current China-related experience.

## Compare the company name across every project document

The most useful checks are often simple comparisons made before a deposit, not dramatic investigations after something has gone wrong.

Put the following documents side by side:

1. The supplier’s quotation or pro forma invoice.
2. The business license.
3. The public registration search result.
4. The purchase order or contract draft.
5. Bank account instructions.
6. Commercial invoice details, if issued before payment.
7. Any company stamp or signed confirmation the supplier asks you to rely on.

The legal Chinese company name and payment recipient should make sense together. The EU SME Centre advises buyers to verify that the company name and business scope on the license match the names used in business contracts, and to verify that a bank account belongs to a bank in the same jurisdiction as the company rather than to an individual.[2]

That does not mean every difference is automatically fraud. A supplier may use a trading company for export documentation, have a separate manufacturing entity, or ask a group company to receive payment. But any difference should be explicit, documented, and understood before you pay. Ask a direct question such as:

> “Please explain the relationship between the company named on the quotation, the entity in the contract, and the account receiving payment. Please confirm which entity is responsible for this order.”

A clear written answer is better than an assumption based on a short message or a verbal explanation.

## Confirm the role of the person you are dealing with

A business license does not automatically show that your sales contact can bind the company to a contract, approve a specification change, or authorize a refund. Ask the supplier to identify who is responsible for commercial confirmation, technical confirmation, and payment instructions.

For a larger order, keep a simple decision record:

- Who sent the quote?
- Who confirmed the final product specification?
- Who approved the sample or packaging artwork?
- Who is named in the contract?
- Who gave the bank details?
- Who will sign or stamp the order documents?

The EU SME Centre specifically recommends checking whether the contact person is authorized to negotiate for the company.[2] In practice, this means you should not let a familiar chat contact replace clear documentation of the entity and the agreement.

## Separate a legal address from a factory-capability question

A registered address is not the same thing as proof of manufacturing capability. Some suppliers operate from an office address, use a trading structure, subcontract part of production, or have a factory at a different location. None of those facts automatically makes the supplier unsuitable. They change the questions you need to ask.

If your decision depends on a claimed factory, request the actual factory address and explain what you need to verify. For example:

- Is the product made there, assembled there, or sourced from another facility?
- Which production steps are completed at that location?
- Can the supplier show the relevant process for your product version?
- Who controls incoming materials, quality checks, and final packing?
- If subcontracting is involved, which entity is accountable to you?

A [factory or supplier check](/en/services/supplier-factory-checks/) can be useful when you have a specific question and a real address. It should be scoped around visible facts and evidence, not a vague request to decide whether an unknown factory is “good.”

## Make payment instructions part of your verification workflow

Payment is a separate control point. Before sending funds, confirm in writing:

- The account holder’s full legal name
- The receiving bank’s country or jurisdiction
- The account currency and payment reference
- Which contract or pro forma invoice the payment relates to
- Whether the receiving entity is the contracting entity
- Why any third-party payment arrangement is necessary

Avoid treating a last-minute bank-detail change as routine. Pause, verify through a previously known communication channel, and record the confirmation. Do not rely only on a forwarded email or an edited invoice.

For first orders, the wider commercial terms matter too. Your payment schedule, product specification, sample status, inspection timing, and [delivery term](/en/blog/incoterms-first-time-china-buyers-what-they-change/) should be documented before payment. A company record cannot compensate for an unclear product agreement.

## What a business-information check cannot tell you

A registration match is valuable, but it has limits. It cannot prove:

- That the supplier can manufacture your exact design or material
- That a sample will match mass production
- That the quoted price includes every requirement
- That capacity will be available on your required timeline
- That the factory will meet destination-market legal or testing requirements
- That all subcontractors, if any, are suitable
- That the final shipment will pass an inspection

The U.S. International Trade Administration describes due diligence as an ongoing effort to evaluate potential partners and reduce exposure to problems, loss, and liability—not a one-time box to tick.[3] Keep verifying the part of the project that matters at each stage.

## A practical pre-deposit sequence

Use this sequence before paying a first deposit to a new supplier:

1. **Freeze the product brief.** State the product version, materials, quantity, packaging, destination, timing, and open questions. Use the [Project Brief Builder](/en/start-project/) if you need a structured first record.
2. **Request the business license and Chinese legal name.** Record the USCC, legal representative, registered address, and stated business scope.
3. **Check the public registration information.** Compare the data with the license and record the date of your search.
4. **Align all commercial documents.** Match the entity named in the quote, purchase order, contract, invoice, and bank instructions.
5. **Clarify every difference in writing.** Ask about trading-company, factory, group-company, or third-party payment arrangements before the payment is made.
6. **Validate the product-specific evidence.** Review the quotation assumptions, sample plan, capability questions, quality checkpoints, and packaging requirements.
7. **Decide what local evidence is needed.** If an address, production process, or sample requires checking, define the exact question for a visit or inspection.
8. **Keep the record.** Store the documents, questions, answers, approved version, and payment confirmation together.

The point is not to create paperwork for its own sake. It is to make the entity, product, money flow, and next decision visible before a problem becomes expensive.
