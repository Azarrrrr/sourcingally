## Packaging is part of the product decision

Packaging often arrives late in a sourcing conversation: after the product, price, and sample have already been discussed. That is risky. A retail box, insert, carton, label, barcode, or shipping mark can change cost, production timing, freight volume, handling, and the buyer’s presentation to the customer.

The goal is not to collect more design files. The goal is to create a packaging record that a supplier can use without guessing which file, version, language, size, or carton instruction is current.

## Separate the packaging layers

A useful review distinguishes the item a customer sees from the packaging that protects and moves the goods.

| Layer | Questions to settle before production |
| --- | --- |
| Selling-unit packaging | What does one customer receive? Confirm material, dimensions, colour, finish, logo position, barcode space, inserts, and sealed or open presentation. |
| Product labels | Which label text, language, size, location, warning, or batch information is required for the actual destination and product? |
| Inner pack | How many selling units sit together before they enter a master carton? Is there protection, a divider, bag, wrap, or accessory pack? |
| Master carton | Confirm carton material, outside dimensions, units per carton, maximum weight, orientation, and protection for the real transport route. |
| Shipping mark | Confirm the buyer name or code, order number, carton count, destination instruction, handling mark, and format the forwarder or warehouse actually needs. |

There is no universal label or carton checklist. Product type, destination, importer role, marketplace rules, and shipping method can all change the requirements. Ask the importer, forwarder, or relevant professional about the actual shipment rather than relying on a supplier’s generic artwork.

## Turn a design into a factory instruction

An image in a chat is not an approval record. For each packaging element, record the file name, version date, dimensions, printing material or finish, approved language, and the person who gave the approval. If the supplier provides a dieline or proof, save it with the product version it supports.

A short instruction can be enough when it is precise: “Use artwork file `Box-v4.pdf`, approved 14 August; matte white box, 220 × 140 × 60 mm; barcode panel on the back; one insert card; twelve units per master carton.” The point is not the wording. It is that the factory can identify one current answer.

## Review the carton as an operational choice

Carton data belongs in both the packaging record and the shipping conversation. Ask for the proposed carton dimensions, gross and net weight, pieces per carton, photos or a packing diagram where useful, and any condition that changes the plan. A larger carton may affect handling and freight; a weak carton may affect damage risk. Those are questions to make visible before a cargo-ready date, not after.

The International Trade Administration notes that a packing list can include package type, package count, weights, marks, and dimensions, while destination requirements may vary.[1] Your supplier, forwarder, and importer should agree on the actual shipment information before documents or cargo are released.

## Do not approve packaging by saying “looks good”

Use a clear outcome for every packaging point:

- **Approved as shown**: the relevant file and physical or photographed version are identified.
- **Approved with changes**: list each change, the owner, and whether another proof is needed.
- **Not approved**: state what is missing or wrong and what will be checked next.
- **Not decided**: keep the item out of a production approval until a named decision is made.

For a new product, packaging should be connected to the [sample approval guide](/en/product-samples/). For a final-order check, connect it to the [quality-control plan](/en/quality-control-plan/). Before the goods move, bring the same carton and mark details into [shipping readiness](/en/shipping-readiness/).
